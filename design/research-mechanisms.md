# How other games do it: mechanisms for The Divide

November 18, 2026. A survey of card and board games that do what `vision.md` asks for: a choice between helping yourself and helping a side, allegiance you can never be sure of, a shared contested middle, allies who are rivals, and the most nuance for the least rules. About 120 games are catalogued (appendix), grouped into twelve mechanism families. Each family is judged against the vision's five-question test, and the strongest are turned into concrete proposals for the Altar.

Rule details marked † were checked against sources in this session; the rest are well-documented classics described from general knowledge and should be checked against their rulebooks before borrowing anything exact.

## The five-question test (from `vision.md`)

1. **Self vs side:** does it make some action better for you and worse for your side, or the reverse?
2. **Uncertain allegiance:** after seeing it, does the table know a little more, but never enough?
3. **The Altar as a contest:** can contributions be read, stolen, faked or doubted?
4. **Allies as rivals:** are the people on your side also the people you must beat?
5. **Checkable on the table:** short text, no memory load?

## The twelve families

### 1. A secret stake in shared pieces

*Heimlich & Co.*†, *A War of Whispers*†, *Hidden Leaders*†, *Kremlin*†, *Royal Turf*†, *Camel Up*†, *The King's Dilemma*†, *Lifeboat*†

Nobody owns the pieces everyone moves; each player secretly profits from some of them.
- In *Heimlich & Co.* each player is secretly one of seven spies, anyone may move any spy, and the spies nobody owns ("free agents") still score, so an unowned spy can win and leave everyone a loser. An advanced rule pays for guessing everyone's identity at the end.
- In *A War of Whispers* every player secretly ranks the five empires by how much they want each to win, everyone commands every empire's armies, and a player may swap two loyalties once, but must reveal the swapped pair.
- In *Hidden Leaders* each leader is secretly allied with two of four factions, and two markers on a shared track decide which side wins.
- *Royal Turf* adds a 0-value betting chip whose only purpose is bluffing.

**For the vision:** the strongest family for "never knowing who your team is". Allegiance is a stake rather than a membership card, so teams overlap and shift. It passes tests 2, 3 and 5 easily. Test 1 depends on how personal income is tied to the shared outcome; test 4 holds when stakes are ranked.

**On the Altar:** Fates become stakes, with **free Fates**: tokens left in the bag still "play", so the Tide can favor nobody at the table. An end-of-game **guess** (name each player's Fate) pays for reading the table. A **once-per-game swap** that must be revealed is a defection with a visible cost, just like your original Turn.

### 2. Teams hidden inside the cards

*Doppelkopf*†, *Sheepshead*†, *Mü & More*†, *Skat*, *Euchre*, *Tichu*

In *Doppelkopf* the two holders of the queens of clubs are partners, and nobody knows who they are until a queen is played. In *Sheepshead* the picker names an ace, and whoever holds it is the secret partner. In *Mü* the auction winner chooses a partner. In *Skat* every hand is one player against the other two, and the teams change every deal. *Euchre*'s maker may "go alone" for a bigger reward.

**For the vision:** these are four-player games, the exact player count you want, and they have kept hidden, shifting partnerships fun for two centuries. The team is defined by a card, so it is learned through play, not announced. Tests 1, 2 and 5 pass outright.

**On the Altar:** put **allegiance in the cards themselves** (see Proposal B). Partners revealed by what reaches the Altar; Euchre's "going alone" is your existing alone-pays-3.

### 3. Identities that move

*Mascarade*†, *Secrets*†, *Coup: Reformation*, *Feed the Kraken*, *Battlestar Galactica* (sleeper agents)

In *Mascarade* role cards are swapped facedown under the table, so players are often unsure of their own role. In *Secrets* team affiliations can change during the game and are always kept secret. *Coup: Reformation* lets a player pay to convert their (public) allegiance.

**For the vision:** allegiance that can move is the strongest answer to "teams are deduced too quickly" at 3–4 players. The risk is chaos: if nobody knows anything, there are no reads (principle 4). Moves should be visible acts with hidden results.

**On the Altar:** a Fate that can change hands *through the Altar* (Proposal B), or a few cards that swap two players' Fates without looking (Serpent and Temptation already do something similar with cards).

### 4. Shared fate, personal credit

*Cutthroat Caverns*†, *The Republic of Rome*†, *Archipelago*, *Dead of Winter*, *CO2: Second Chance*, *Galaxy Trucker*

In *Cutthroat Caverns* everyone fights the same monster (or everyone dies), but only the killing blow earns prestige. In *The Republic of Rome* the players must keep Rome alive or all lose, while competing for influence. One critic argues its threats and negotiation make games self-balance into anticlimax.

**For the vision:** the purest public-goods games. They teach two lessons:
- **Credit for the decisive contribution** turns contribution into a race rather than a sacrifice.
- **A shared loss condition** must be real and visible, or players free-ride on it.

**On the Altar:** a small credit for the card that *decided* the Tide (the last one Offered to the winning side), or a shared catastrophe if the Altar is starved.

### 5. Elimination thresholds

*High Society*, *Secrets*† (the Hippie wins only with the lowest score)

In *High Society* the player left with the least money at the end is eliminated before scoring, however many points they bought. One threshold rule makes every bid a calculation about everyone else's spending, not just your own.

**For the vision:** turned around, the same structure fixes free-riding without being a patch. *High Society* punishes the biggest spender; The Divide would punish the smallest giver. It changes what "self" means: keeping everything is no longer safe. Passes tests 1, 4 and 5; leaves 2 and 3 alone.

**On the Altar:** **the Miser's Rule** (Proposal A): the player who contributed least to the Altar cannot win.

### 6. Every action helps everyone; the chooser gets a privilege

*Puerto Rico*†, *San Juan*, *Race for the Galaxy*, *Glory to Rome*, *Innovation*, *Twilight Imperium* (strategy cards)

When a role is chosen in *Puerto Rico*, every player takes that action and the chooser gets a small privilege. Players are always involved, and choosing well means helping yourself more than you help others.

**For the vision:** a public-goods mechanism built into the turn. Every action is a gift to the table and an advantage to you; the art is choosing the action that helps you most and your rivals least. Passes 1 and 5; weak on 2.

**On the Altar:** "follow" rules: when you Offer, others on your side may follow with a card of the same alignment (helping the side), but only the leader of the Offer earns the privilege.

### 7. Investment and control of shared entities

*Imperial*†, *Pax Pamir*, *Acquire*, *Chicago Express*, *18xx*, *Tammany Hall*, *Modern Art*, *Medici*

In *Imperial* no player owns a nation. Players buy bonds, and whoever holds the most of a nation's debt controls it until someone outbids them; nations' success pays all their investors. *Pax Pamir* pays a dominant coalition's loyalists by their influence.

**For the vision:** contributions earn a stake, which softens free-riding without making the shared good private. Control shifts naturally, and allies are rivals for the controlling share. Passes 1 and 4; usually public, so weak on 2.

**On the Altar:** your contributions to a side over the game become your share of that side's success (tested as Dominance, Rounds 22 and 22b).

### 8. Hidden commitments, revealed together

*El Grande*†, *Battlestar Galactica*, *Diplomacy*, *Libertalia*, *Citadels*, *Skull*

In *El Grande* players secretly add knights to the Castillo, announcing only how many, then reveal a secret destination disc together. *Battlestar Galactica* shuffles hidden skill-check contributions with random Destiny cards so sabotage is deniable.

**For the vision:** the Altar already is this (facedown Offers revealed at Evening; the Omen is the Destiny deck). The lesson: **announce quantity, hide quality.** Everyone sees *that* you Offered; nobody sees what.

### 9. Alliances offered, accepted and rewarded per conflict

*Cosmic Encounter*†, *Dune*†, *Oath*†, *Twilight Imperium*, *Game of Thrones: The Board Game*, *Root*, *Inis*

In *Cosmic Encounter* both sides of every fight invite allies, who commit ships and share the outcome; defending allies get their own reward, which is what keeps defenders from being abandoned. In *Dune* allies win together, and every player secretly holds traitor cards for enemy leaders. In *Oath* the Chancellor can grant citizenship to exiles, and citizens share in the empire's victory but can also succeed it.

**For the vision:** alliances that are *formed in the open each conflict* create the talk the vision wants (principle 7), and different rewards for each side of the alliance keep the weaker side attractive.

**On the Altar:** a Morning invitation: the first player declares a side and invites partners; accepting gives a small bonus if that side wins, and is a public promise that a facedown Offer can betray.

### 10. Secret objectives and third interests

*Oath* (Visions)†, *Shadow Hunters* (Neutrals)†, *Lifeboat*†, *Nemesis*, *Dead of Winter*, *The King's Dilemma*†

Players who win by a different condition (stalemate, a specific rival's ruin, a third outcome) break two-team symmetry and make any read uncertain.

**For the vision:** your original Forsaken is exactly this. At 3–4 players a third interest is what keeps "two sides" from being solved by counting.

### 11. Trading, binding and non-binding

*Chinatown*†, *Sidereal Confluence*†, *Bohnanza*, *Catan*, *Intrigue*, *Quo Vadis*, *Pit*, *Junta*

*Sidereal Confluence*'s trades are binding, including promises of future delivery; *Chinatown* is almost pure negotiation. *Intrigue* and *Junta* run on bribes and broken promises.

**For the vision:** this is the missing tooth of negotiation (vision tension "negotiation with nothing to trade"). Something must be exchangeable for talk to be more than persuasion.

**On the Altar:** cards from hand may be traded by agreement. Cards are votes, stakes and engine parts at once, so every trade is political.

### 12. Scoring your weakest side

*Tigris & Euphrates*†, *Ingenious*

Your score is your lowest color, so you must feed every color you rely on.

**For the vision:** an elegant answer to hedging. A player cannot specialize, so they must sometimes help the other side, which makes allegiance shift on its own. It cuts against firm teams, so it fits the Forsaken more than the base game.

## Proposals for The Divide

Ranked by how well they serve the vision for what they cost to learn. A, B and C are worth prototyping in the lab now; D to F need people.

### A. The Miser's Rule (High Society's threshold, turned around)

> At the end of the game, the player who placed the fewest cards on the Altar cannot win.

- **Serves:** self vs side (principle 1) and allies as rivals (4).
- **Why:** the lab shows Offers are under-supplied when they are a pure public good (about 20% of actions under multiplied Fates). This keeps Offers a pure contribution, without paying for them, yet makes refusing to contribute a gamble. Everyone wants to contribute just enough not to be last.
- **Cost:** one sentence; a public tally (a token on the Altar's edge for each Offer, or simply a count kept face up).

### B. Oaths that pass through the Altar (from *Doppelkopf* and *Mascarade*)

> Your allegiance is an Oath card you hold in hand (Light or Dark). You may Offer it like any card: it counts double on the Altar. Whoever ends the Day holding it in their Choir or hand takes that allegiance.

- **Serves:** uncertain allegiance (2), the Altar as a contest (3), and switching sides with a cost (the Turn, rebuilt).
- **Why:** allegiance becomes a physical thing that can be risked, stolen, faked and traded on the Altar itself. Offering your Oath is the biggest possible vote for your side and the biggest possible risk to your own place in it. Gathering someone's Oath off the Altar is defection by theft.
- **Cost:** one card type and one rule; Fate tokens are no longer needed.

### C. Free Fates and the final guess (from *Heimlich & Co.*)

> Fates left in the bag are free: a free Fate's side can win the Tide and pay nobody. At the end, each player secretly writes down every other player's Fate: 1 Glory for each correct guess.

- **Serves:** reads, not luck (principle 4); never knowing your team (2).
- **Why:** rewards reading the table and bluffing it, and keeps leftover tokens meaningful (your original "there may not even be a Forsaken").
- **Cost:** a scorepad line.

### D. Decisive Offer credit (from *Cutthroat Caverns*)

> When the Altar is revealed, the last card Offered to the winning side earns its owner 1 Glory.

- **Serves:** self vs side (1). Turns late contribution into a race and gives the last player a reason to commit rather than wait.
- **Risk:** makes the last seat stronger; needs a turn-order check.

### E. Morning invitations (from *Cosmic Encounter*)

> In the Morning, the first player names a side and invites others. Each who accepts gains 1 if that side wins the Tide, and loses 1 if it loses.

- **Serves:** talk (7) and allies as rivals (4). Public promises that facedown Offers can betray. Needs people to judge.

### F. Trading cards from hand (from *Chinatown* and *Sidereal Confluence*)

> At any time, players may exchange cards from hand by agreement. Promises about the future are not binding.

- **Serves:** negotiation with teeth (7).
- **Risk:** slower play; needs people to judge.

## How this connects to the lab

- **Multiplied Fates** (Rounds 25–26) is family 1. It already measures best against the vision; Proposal A answers its free-riding and Proposal C its readability.
- **Dominance** (Round 22b) is family 7.
- **The collection plate** (Round 24) is closest to family 4, but pays Offers privately, which the vision warns against.
- **Next:** Proposal A on multiplied Fates, then Proposal B as its own core.
