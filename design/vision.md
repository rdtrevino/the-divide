# The Divide: the vision

What Robert is building, distilled from the whole history: the first rulebook drafts (July 2026), every version from V28 to V44, the decisions recorded in the changelog and design notes, and Robert's own words in the design conversations of November 2026. It is meant as a charter: the test any future rule has to pass.

## In one sentence

**A game where every card you play is a choice between helping yourself and helping a side you can never be sure is yours.**

The first rulebook said it this way, and it still holds:

> "To win, you need two things: the world must belong to your secret faction, and among the players of that faction, you must hold the most Glory. Faction victory makes you eligible. Glory makes you the winner. Neither is enough on its own, and **that gap is where the game lives**: you must help your side win the world while quietly out-earning the very allies you are helping."
> — Rulebook v2 (pre-rework)

> "Push the world in public, position yourself in private, get paid when the two line up."
> — the same draft

And in November 2026:

> "The central tension of a card that helps you being offered would be less advantageous for you but maybe helpful for everyone, and pulling from the middle would either be more hurtful for you, by adding a card to your Choir that you didn't necessarily need but was helpful to get rid of, or take a card that was helpful to you but hurts the Altar overall. A true game theory focused game."

> "The public-goods dilemma and the help yourself vs help the team (but also not ever really knowing who your team is) dilemma."

## The core: one dilemma, seen three ways

Everything in the original design is built on a single social dilemma, the one economists call a **public-goods game**: what helps the group costs the individual, and every player is tempted to let the others pay.

| Action | Helps your side | Helps you | The dilemma |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Offer** a card of your side's color | Pushes the shared Altar toward your side | Gives up a card that could have built your own Choir | Contribute, or let your allies contribute while you build |
| **Gather** a card of the enemy's color | Removes the enemy's vote from the Altar | Puts a card you didn't want in your Choir | Sacrifice yourself for the side |
| **Gather** a card of your own color | Removes your own side's vote | Strengthens your Choir | Take for yourself at the side's expense |

Two further layers turn the dilemma into a *game* rather than a puzzle:

1. **You never quite know who your team is.** Fates are secret, leftover tokens stay in the bag, so even the size of each side is unknown. Your public Choir is a claim, not a proof. "Every card you play is both a move and a message, and the players who win are the ones who make the table read the message wrong."
2. **Allies are rivals.** Winning as a side only makes you *eligible*; within the side, the one who gave the least and kept the most wins. This is what makes alliances tense instead of comfortable, and it is the same structure Pax Pamir uses (coalition dominance pays its loyalists by their personal influence).

## Core principles

In order of importance. Each one comes with the evidence from the history: decisions Robert made, ideas he kept coming back to, and what he rejected.

### 1. Self versus side, every turn

Every action should help either you or your side, rarely both, and the best players are the ones who know when to switch.

- The original win condition (faction first, then most Glory within it).
- The Gather text in the first rulebook: "Stealing a Dark card off the Altar both strengthens your Choir and weakens the Dark Tide in a single action."
- V41: Robert's goal was "every turn a real choice between building a Choir that pays out more and more, and Offering to sway the Altar". Free Exalt (V42) was adopted to make that choice real (Exalt went from 1% to 27% of actions).

### 2. Uncertain allegiance

Teams should be real but never certain: not their members, not their size, not even permanent.

- Secret Fates; leftover tokens hidden "so no one knows the true headcount of any faction"; a Forsaken who may not even be in the game.
- The Turn: defect once, at a cost, with a scar that stays in your Choir.
- Robert's interest in Pax Pamir: "they do allegiance but also switching sides very well".
- **Secret personal objectives were rejected for the Open Game because "they work the way Fates already do"**: the Fate is the hidden objective, and nothing should duplicate it.

### 3. The Altar is the shared, contested middle

One public pool that everyone feeds and anyone can raid. It is the commons of the public-goods game: contributions are provisional, can be stolen, and are judged together at the end of the Day.

- "Nothing on it is safe or permanent: cards placed there can be taken by anyone before the Day ends."
- First Fruits (V35): everyone contributes one honest card each Morning.
- Facedown Offers (V35): the contribution is hidden until the reveal, so you are trusted, not watched.

### 4. Hidden information creates reads, not luck

"Chaos and choice over automatic play. Hidden information should create reads and bluffs, not just luck."

- The face-up row and "draw 2, keep 1" were rejected because they made play automatic.
- The pieces Robert keeps returning to are all bluff tools: facedown Offers, First Fruits as the one honest card, Mirage, Veil, Vessel, and the Omen and wagers he liked in the rework.

### 5. Engines that plug into the tension

Building a Choir should make you stronger as an individual (the "help yourself" half of the dilemma) and should matter more the longer you invest. It should never replace the Altar as the heart of the game.

- The growing Choir, free Exalt, stores, sets and growth cards (V41 to V43).
- **Synergy by alignment, not by named families** (Robert rejected families in V41). Light cards help Light, Dark cards help Dark, so the engine and the side are the same axis. That is what makes building your Choir a statement about which side you are on.
- His own coalition rule from V41, where the stronger lean in a pair takes more, links Choir strength to standing within your side.

### 6. The most strategy and nuance for the least complexity

"The most strategy and nuance with the least complexity." And: "Never operate to fix patches. Work from the ground up."

- Robert's rule for card text: no memory load, short text, everything checkable on the table.
- Mirror pairs (Raphael and Azazel, Seraphim and Legion, Uriel and Beelzebub, Providence and Mammon): one idea, learned once, used twice.
- The question to ask of every rule: does it create a decision, or only a procedure?

### 7. Talk is part of the game

"Open negotiation is permitted at all times, and the game expects you to use it. Promises are worthless, which makes them valuable."

- Deals, threats and promises are open and non-binding.
- Glory gifts (to seal a deal) existed originally. Robert removed them in V40, so the only thing that changes hands is a card, and only when a card says so.

### 8. Every game starts somewhere different

The random starting Choir "steers each game's strategy": Robert chose a random deal over a chosen one in V40, even though the chosen one balanced better. Variety comes from the deal and the cards, not from setup choices.

### 9. Fair, and measured

Every card between 0.75 and 1.25 in testing (Robert's target since V38); mirrored Light and Dark halves of the deck; "no profit from playing only one alignment". Robert balances by evidence and cares that no card or seat is a trap.

## The mechanisms, sorted

| Mechanism | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The Altar and the Tide | **Load-bearing** | The commons and its verdict. Everything else points at it. |
| Offer (hidden contribution) | **Load-bearing** | The "help the side" move. |
| Gather (raid the middle) | **Load-bearing** | The double-edged move in both directions of the dilemma. |
| Exalt (build privately) | **Load-bearing** | The "help yourself" move. |
| The Choir (public, built from your side's color) | **Load-bearing** | Personal strength, and your public claim of allegiance. |
| Secret allegiance (Fates) | **Load-bearing, currently missing** | The "never knowing your team" half. Lost when the 3-player variant became the core game. |
| Rank within the side ("most Glory among the winners") | **Load-bearing, currently missing** | Makes allies rivals. Lost with the Fates. |
| Defection (the Turn) | **Core flavor, currently missing** | Switching sides with a lasting cost (the Pax Pamir quality Robert admires). |
| First Fruits | **Strong support** | Forces one honest contribution a Day; the read that starts every bluff. |
| Facedown Offers, the Omen, wagers | **Strong support** | Deniability: a surprising Tide could be sabotage or luck. |
| The 69 cards (abilities) | **Support (the engine and the variety)** | Loved, and they should stay, but as a pool that feeds the dilemma, not as the dilemma itself. |
| Glory as score and currency | **Support** | Stores, costs and the race. |
| The Forsaken (wins when the world stays balanced) | **Dormant idea worth keeping** | A third interest that profits from stalemate; it makes "nobody wins the Tide" a real outcome someone is playing for. |
| Celestials and Events | **Optional layer** | Asymmetry and variety; never the core. |
| Coalition crowding rules, the Crown, Reaping losses | **Patches** | Each was added in the open 3–4 player game to fix a symptom that the hidden-team structure handled by itself. |

## How the game drifted, and why it matters now

1. **July 2026 (the original):** 4–6 players, secret Fates, the Balance, the Turn, faction victory plus most Glory within the faction. The full vision.
2. **The 3-player variant:** "No secrets, no factions, no mercy." The Fates, the Balance and the Turn were set aside so three friends could play. It was meant as a variant.
3. **V33 (November 13):** the variant became **the Open Game, the core rules**, and Hidden Roles was pushed to 5–6 players. From here on, every change was made to the open game.
4. **V34 to V44:** a long tuning loop against bots. It produced real gains: First Fruits, facedown Offers, free Exalt, the growing Choir, a fair deck, the website. But the open game had no hidden teams, so the team-versus-self tension had to be manufactured with scoring rules (coalition crowding, mirrored losses), and each rule created a new symptom to patch.
5. **The rework (November 18):** wagers, crowding, the Crown and the Omen gave the open game new tension. But the wager pays you personally for Offering, which **turns the public good into a private good** and removes the original dilemma. That is why it never quite felt like the game Robert set out to make.

**The real design problem is not "how do we balance the open game".** It is: **how do we keep secret, shifting teams and the self-versus-side dilemma at 3 and 4 players**, where the original needed 5 or 6. The rest follows from that answer.

## Tensions inside the vision, to resolve on purpose

- **Hidden teams at 3–4 players.** With two sides and three or four players, teams are tiny and quickly deduced. Possible answers: unknown headcount (draw from a larger bag, as the original did); allegiance that can change mid-game (the Turn, or cards that swap Fates, as in *Secrets*); or a secret *stake* instead of a secret team (*A War of Whispers*, where every player secretly backs several sides by different amounts).
- **Negotiation with nothing to trade.** Robert removed Glory gifts, which kept kingmaking out but left deals as cheap talk. Something tradeable (cards from hand, or a promise that the game can enforce, such as Council or Covenant) would give negotiation teeth.
- **Stakes versus a rewarding table.** Robert wants leaning hard to carry risk, and wants 4-player games to feel rewarding rather than punishing. Losses on the Reaping caused herding in every test; the risk works better as a lost contribution (a card given to a side that lost) or a hidden stake that can sink.
- **Every card versus least complexity.** Robert loves all 69 cards; a light game cannot ask players to learn 69 texts at once. A selection per game (a Pantheon) keeps both.
- **Engine versus Tide.** Building should be the "help yourself" half of the dilemma, so its payoff should depend on your side (or your secret side) winning. Otherwise it becomes a separate game beside the Altar.

## The test for any new rule

A rule belongs in The Divide if it does at least one of these, and costs less to learn than it gives:

1. **It sharpens self versus side:** it makes some action better for you and worse for your side, or the reverse.
2. **It keeps allegiance uncertain:** after seeing it, the table knows a little more, but never enough.
3. **It turns the Altar into a contest:** contributions can be read, stolen, faked or doubted.
4. **It makes an ally a rival:** the people on your side are also the people you must beat.
5. **It is checkable on the table:** no memory load, short text.

A rule that only fixes a symptom (a cap, a catch-up, a tiebreak) should be the last resort, and a sign that the core is missing something.
