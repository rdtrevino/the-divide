# 1. Direction: one idea, carried everywhere

A direction is not a mood. It is one sentence that rules things out, and a written contract for the first screen.
Most bland work fails here: it has a palette and a font but no idea, so every section defaults to the category.

## 1. Find the idea that carries meaning

The strongest systems turn one truth about the subject into a visual rule that **encodes information**, so the design
can't be moved to another product. Examples of the shape (not to copy):
- A shared planner: "two lives, overlapping": each person is a colour, their overlap is a third colour, and that blend
  marks shared free time everywhere (calendar, buttons, the logo).
- A studio named for a cut circle: "the cut is the layout's axis, the offset is the drop between halves, the right
  half arrives a half-beat late": grid, type placement and motion all come from the mark's own numbers.

Test: name the idea in one sentence. If it doesn't exclude at least three common choices, it isn't an idea yet.

## 2. Refuse the rut, then derive from the audience's world

1. Write the **category default** (the page this category always ships) and its **predictable opposite**. Both are the
   rut; neither may be a candidate.
2. List **seven concrete systems from the audience's culture**: artifacts, places, rituals, print and screen
   traditions they know by heart (record sleeves, scoreboards, transit maps, programmes, posters, field guides). Each
   gets one line on why it can carry the product's mechanism and one on how it looks (ground, colours, type voices,
   the one signature). Spread them across at least three material families; if more than three share one family,
   keep digging. Spend at most one candidate on the literal reading of the product name.
3. Ask what the product would be as a physical object, and what its world looked like before the web.
4. Pick the strongest by **audience recognition** and **product clarity**. From each rejected candidate, take its one
   discipline the winner lacks (a palette's total commitment, a grid's density, a structure's honesty), never its
   clothes (a font, a shadow, a motif).

For a pinned brand (fixed colours and faces), the seven candidates are **compositions and rituals**, not palettes:
how the pinned world can stage this surface.

## 3. Calibrate against the machine-made looks

Generated interfaces cluster around a few looks whatever the subject. Read `06-tells.md` before choosing. If someone
could guess your aesthetic from the category alone, rework until they couldn't. If your brand legitimately owns one of
those ingredients (a cream ground, a display serif, a warm accent), the composition must earn it: midnight fields,
real work, the mark's geometry as structure, scale contrast no template uses.

## 4. Write the direction contract (~150 words, six blocks)

Write it in the PR or a surface brief, never in shipped code.

- **THESIS**: the one idea this surface owns, and the category-default arrangement it refuses.
- **OWN-WORLD**: palette strategy and component language, specific enough to recognise with all content removed.
- **STORY**: what the visitor understands, believes and does, in order down the page.
- **FIRST VIEWPORT**: the exact composition: what is where, at what scale, and where the primary action sits.
- **SIGNATURE**: the one interaction or motion moment that only this product would have (with its no-script and
  reduced-motion state).
- **FINISH**: "unreviewed and undocumented is unfinished": the build ends with the separate review and DESIGN.md.

If a block reads like a mood ("calm, premium, warm"), the direction isn't decided yet.

## 5. The first viewport is a thesis, not a header

- **The memory test:** if someone left after one screen, what would they describe an hour later? If the honest answer
  is a mood, the concept hasn't committed.
- Demonstrate the mechanism at the scale it has in life: the work, the product at work, the idea dramatised. Do not
  trap it inside a standard hero (centred headline, grey subline, two buttons, blob) or a split hero over a row of
  cards: that is the template whatever world paints it.
- The primary action works in its real form in the first viewport.

## 6. Colour strategy before colours

Pick one before picking hues:
- **Restrained**: tinted neutrals and one accent; colour does jobs (action, state). Common for Operate and Read.
- **Committed**: one saturated colour carries 30–60% of the surface.
- **Full palette**: 3–4 named roles, each with a job.
- **Drenched**: the surface is the colour.

Colour commits at page scale: fields that own whole regions, not accents scattered on a neutral ground. Choose light or
dark from a one-sentence physical scene (who, where, under what light), never by category; follow the device when the
product must work in both.

## 7. Present, then build

On a new world, present the direction plus up to three real alternates (each with the same anatomy: thesis, palette,
materials, first viewport, honest risk) and the category standard as a quiet, honest option. The person picks. Build
the picked direction at full commitment; never a safer interpretation of it.
